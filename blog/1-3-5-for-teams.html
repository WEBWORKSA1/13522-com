Running a team on 1-3-5

By the 13522 editorial team · 7-minute read

Most teams don't suffer from laziness. They suffer from fog: too many priorities, unclear ownership and a steady stream of interruptions that make deep work impossible. The 1-3-5 method, extended with focus sprints and reviews, gives a team a shared language for cutting through that fog.

The one rule: everyone names a Big task

At the start of each day, every person names one Big task — a single outcome they intend to finish. That's the only thing shared at stand-up. Medium and small tasks stay personal.

This has three effects. Managers see priorities instead of activity. Misalignment becomes visible in seconds ("wait, I thought the launch was the priority?"). And people feel permission to protect time for their most important work.

The 60-second stand-up

Each person answers two questions: What's my Big task today? and Is anything blocking it? Discussion of blockers happens afterwards with only the people involved. A team of eight finishes in under ten minutes.

For remote or distributed teams, run it async: a channel or thread where each person posts their Big task by a set time. Managers respond only to blockers.

Shared focus windows

Individual focus sprints are fragile when colleagues can interrupt at any moment. Agree on one or two shared windows — for example 9:30–11:00 and 14:00–15:00 — with no meetings and an expectation that messages wait. Urgent issues have a defined escalation route (a phone call, not a chat message).

The daily close

At the end of the day each person posts a short update: Big task done or not, what carries over, any blocker for tomorrow, and tomorrow's Big task. This replaces most status meetings and creates a lightweight written record of progress.

What managers should and shouldn't do

  • Do check that Big tasks connect to team goals. If they don't, that's a conversation about priorities, not performance.
  • Do protect focus windows yourself — the fastest way to kill them is a manager who schedules over them.
  • Don't use daily scores as performance metrics. The moment scores are judged, people will under-plan to look good.
  • Don't ask for everyone's full nine-item list. It turns a clarity tool into surveillance.

Weekly review as a team

On Friday, spend 20 minutes as a team looking at the week: which Big tasks shipped, what kept slipping, and which meetings could be shortened or removed. Choose next week's top priorities together so Monday's Big tasks are easy to pick.

Rolling it out

Start with a two-week trial. Explain the why (clarity, focus, fewer status meetings), agree on focus windows, and run the 60-second stand-up from day one. At the end of the trial, ask the team what to keep and what to change. Most teams keep the stand-up and the focus windows and adapt the rest.
