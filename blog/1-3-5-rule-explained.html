Method

The 1-3-5 rule, explained properly

By the 13522 editorial team · 8-minute read

The 1-3-5 rule is one of the simplest planning methods ever shared: each day, commit to one big task, three medium tasks and five small tasks. It spread through productivity blogs, app makers and business coaches because it fixes the problem nearly everyone has — a to-do list that is longer than the day.

Simple doesn't mean easy, though. Most people who try 1-3-5 and give up do so for the same few reasons. This article explains the method properly, including the parts that usually get skipped.

The shape, not the number

It's tempting to treat 1-3-5 as a quota: nine boxes to fill. The real value is the shape. One big slot forces you to name the single outcome that makes the day worthwhile. Three medium slots acknowledge that real work has supporting pieces. Five small slots give the inevitable admin a container, so it can't sprawl across the whole day.

The shape also reflects a realistic budget of attention. Most knowledge workers get somewhere between three and five hours of deep, focused work in a day once meetings, messages and breaks are subtracted. A big task of around two hours, three medium tasks of around an hour and five small tasks of ten minutes each fits that budget with a little slack.

How to size a task

Sizing is where the method succeeds or fails. Use these working definitions:

  • Big: 90 minutes to 3 hours of focused effort with a clear finish line.
  • Medium: 30 to 90 minutes. Important, but not the day's headline.
  • Small: under 15 minutes. Usually a single action — a reply, a payment, a booking.

If a task feels bigger than three hours, it isn't a task, it's a project. Your Big task is the next chunk of that project that can be finished today. "Launch the website" becomes "Write and publish the pricing page".

If a "small" task keeps taking 40 minutes, it was never small. "Sort out the inbox" is a medium task disguised as a small one. "Reply to the three client emails flagged yesterday" is genuinely small.

Write outcomes, not activities

"Work on the report" has no end; you can work on a report forever. "Send the draft report to Maria" has a finish line you can cross before lunch. Writing tasks as outcomes is the single fastest way to make a 1-3-5 list more effective, because each item becomes something you can actually tick off.

What to do with everything else

Your list of possible tasks will always exceed nine. That's fine. Keep a master list (or let the planner's Smart Sort "park" extras) and pick from it each evening. When something urgent arrives mid-day, it replaces a medium task rather than being added on top. The list never grows beyond nine; that constraint is the whole point.

Carry-overs are information

Unfinished tasks will happen. Move them to tomorrow without guilt — but notice patterns. If the same item carries over three days in a row, one of four things is true: it's unclear, it's bigger than you thought, it isn't actually important, or you're avoiding it. Each has a different fix: rewrite it, split it, drop it, or schedule it as tomorrow's first focus sprint.

Why we added "2 and 2"

On 13522.com we extend the classic rule with two focus sprints and two short reviews. In practice, the list alone tells you what to do but not when. Two protected sprints — 25 to 90 minutes each with notifications off — make sure the Big task actually gets the attention it needs. A three-minute morning review and a five-minute evening review keep the system honest and make the next day's plan better than today's.

Try it for two weeks

Any planning system needs time before it feels natural. Run 1-3-5 for ten working days, score each day, and then look at the pattern: which tasks carried over, which days went well, and what those days had in common. You'll learn more about how you work from those ten scores than from any productivity book.
