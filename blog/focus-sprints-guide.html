Focus sprints: 25, 50 or 90 minutes?

By the 13522 editorial team · 6-minute read

A focus sprint is a fixed block of time dedicated to one task, with distractions removed. It's the engine of the 13522 method: two sprints a day are usually enough to move your Big task meaningfully forward. The question people ask most is how long a sprint should be.

25 minutes: the on-ramp

Twenty-five minutes, made famous by the Pomodoro Technique, is short enough that starting feels easy. It's ideal when you're tired, procrastinating, returning from a break or doing work you find aversive. The goal of a 25-minute sprint is often just to begin; momentum does the rest.

50 minutes: the workhorse

For most knowledge work — writing, analysis, design, coding — 50 minutes gives enough time to load the problem into your head and make real progress, while still ending before fatigue sets in. A 10-minute break afterwards keeps the rhythm sustainable.

90 minutes: deep work

Complex creative or technical problems often need 20–30 minutes just to get fully immersed. Ninety-minute sprints suit experienced practitioners and demanding work. Use them sparingly — one 90-minute sprint plus one 50-minute sprint is a strong day.

Rules that make sprints work

  • One task. Decide what the sprint is for before you press start.
  • Notifications off. Phone in another room if possible, chat set to do-not-disturb.
  • One window. Close tabs and apps unrelated to the task.
  • A parking list. When a stray thought appears ("I must email Tom"), write it down and return to work.
  • A real break. Stand up, move, look at something far away. Scrolling is not a break.

When to schedule sprints

Put sprint one at your highest-energy time — for many people that's the first two to three hours of the working day. Protect it like a meeting by blocking it on your calendar. Sprint two can go late morning or mid-afternoon.

What if I'm interrupted?

Real urgencies happen. If you must stop, note where you were in one sentence so restarting is easy, deal with the interruption, and restart the timer from zero rather than resuming. If interruptions are constant, that's a team or environment problem worth raising — see running a team on 1-3-5.
