The complete guide to the 13522 method

Updated regularly · 12-minute read

The 13522 method is a daily planning system built on the popular 1-3-5 to-do list: one big task, three medium tasks and five small tasks. It adds two habits that make the list actually get done — two protected focus sprints and two short reviews.

1. What the 1-3-5 list is

The 1-3-5 rule is a widely shared productivity technique: each day you commit to one major task, three medium tasks and five small tasks. That's nine items — enough to feel productive, few enough to finish. It has been written about by productivity apps, time-tracking companies and business coaches for years because it solves the most common planning problem: lists that are too long to ever complete.

The method doesn't tell you what to work on. It forces you to choose. When there is only one Big slot, you can't pretend that ten things are top priority.

2. Why a fixed shape works

Decision fatigue. Every time you look at a 30-item list you re-decide what to do next. A short, pre-sorted list removes those micro-decisions so energy goes into the work.

Completion bias. Finishing things feels good, and that feeling fuels the next task. Five small tasks give you guaranteed wins; the Big task gives the day meaning.

Honest capacity. A typical knowledge worker has three to five hours of genuinely focused time per day once meetings, messages and breaks are accounted for. One big task (about two hours), three medium (about three hours) and five small (about one hour) is ambitious but realistic.

Context switching. Grouping similar-sized tasks lets you batch: deep work in sprints, small admin in a single block.

3. Sizing tasks honestly

TierTypical lengthExamples
Big (1)90 min – 3 hWrite a proposal, ship a feature, record a video, study a chapter
Medium (3)30 – 90 minReview a document, prepare a meeting, edit a draft, plan a campaign
Small (5)under 15 minReply to a message, pay a bill, book an appointment, file a receipt

Two rules keep sizing honest. First, anything over three hours isn't a task — it's a project, and today's Big task is its next meaningful chunk. Second, write tasks as outcomes ("Send v1 of the pricing page to Alex") rather than activities ("Work on pricing"). Outcomes have finish lines; activities expand to fill the day.

If you aren't sure, use the task time estimator. It applies a buffer for the planning fallacy — our tendency to underestimate our own tasks — and tells you which tier the task belongs in.

4. The two focus sprints

A list without protected time is a wish list. The 13522 method schedules two focus sprints: uninterrupted blocks of 25, 50 or 90 minutes with notifications off and only the relevant window open.

  • Sprint one goes to the Big task, ideally in the first half of the day.
  • Sprint two finishes the Big task or tackles the most important Medium task.
  • Take a real break between sprints — stand up, walk, get water. Screens don't count.

Twenty-five minutes suits days when focus is hard. Fifty minutes suits most knowledge work. Ninety minutes suits deep creative or technical work once you're practised. The planner includes a timer that marks each sprint complete automatically.

5. The two reviews

Morning (3 minutes). Check your calendar, confirm the nine tasks, and write one sentence: "Today is a win if…". Add one line about what might get in the way and what you'll do if it happens.

Evening (5 minutes). Tick off what's done. For anything unfinished, decide: carry over, delegate, or drop. Then choose tomorrow's Big task. This single habit — deciding tomorrow's Big task tonight — is the highest-leverage part of the system.

On Fridays, extend the evening review into a weekly review: look at the week's scores, notice what keeps carrying over, and pick next week's five Big tasks.

7. Adapting it to your life

  • Meeting-heavy days: scale to 1·2·3. The Big task stays.
  • Students: the Big task is one study session with a clear target ("practice exam section B").
  • Parents and caregivers: run a lighter home list in parallel, or use the five small slots for household admin.
  • Shift workers: plan per shift rather than per calendar day; the evening review becomes your end-of-shift handover.
  • Teams: each person shares only their Big task at stand-up. See running a team on 1-3-5.

8. Common mistakes

  • Two Big tasks. If both feel essential, one of them is tomorrow's.
  • Inflated small tasks. "Clean up the inbox" is not small. "Reply to the three client emails" is.
  • No minutes. Without estimates you can't see when the plan is impossible.
  • Skipping the evening review. The next morning starts with decisions instead of work.
  • Treating carry-overs as failure. They're data. Three in a row means the task needs redefining, delegating or dropping.

9. Start today

Open the free planner, paste your list into Smart Sort, and choose your Big task. Run one sprint before lunch. Tonight, do the five-minute review and pick tomorrow's Big task. That's the whole system.
